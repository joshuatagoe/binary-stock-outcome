## Summary

This group checks what a grader can verify about Meridian. It covers the languages and tools (D4), the blockchain (D18), the tests (D30), whether a fake-price "simulation mode" is needed (D11), and how the project copes with Solana's devnet, the public test network where all money is play money (A2, Q23, Q24, Q55). Most of the plan survives the evidence. The stack, the chain and the test plan hold, but each needs a fix in its write-up. HyperLiquid now runs its own daily yes/no markets, called HIP-4 (F6.03), so D4's one-line "not explored" note would be out of date. D4 also still names Next.js, although Q26 deferred that choice. D30 misses a few tests: a second settle must fail, the work limits per transaction need checking, and so does a bot crash. D11's "no simulation mode" holds, because the spec requires the settle step to keep its price checks (F6.06). However, the plan describes the line between the banned simulation mode and the allowed local fake-clock run in three different ways. The evidence pulls against only one item, A2: the owner assumes a devnet outage won't count against the project. The spec's liveness rule says "every trading day" and allows no exception (F6.18), and no source found says graders will excuse a lost day. Of the open questions, the owner's direction on Q24 holds with one gap: at a weekend, a grader can't rerun the cycle on devnet itself. Q23 and Q55 need the owner's choice. The weakest evidence is what this review did not find: the graders' rubric, an official record of devnet outages, and what comparable projects do for demos.

## Briefings

### Q23. How does a day lost to a devnet outage count?

**1. The terms.**
- **Liveness** is the spec's measure that the system does its daily job. Markets must be created before the 9:30 AM open and settled within 10 minutes of the 4:00 PM close, "every trading day" (F6.18). A **trading day** is a weekday when the US stock market is open.
- To **settle** is to record which side won, using the stock's closing price from the oracle. To **redeem** is to swap a winning token for $1.00.
- In a **devnet halt**, Solana's test network stops making blocks for a while. Everything on it is frozen but kept.
- In a **ledger reset**, the network is wiped and restarted from empty, and programs, markets and balances on it are gone. Solana's own docs say devnet "may be subject to ledger resets" (F6.15).
- **Force majeure** means an outside event you couldn't control, used as a reason to leave a day out of the count.

**2. Worked example.** On a Tuesday, Bob mints 10 pairs: he locks $10.00 of test USDC and gets 10 Yes and 10 No on "META ≥ $680 today?". He then sells the 10 Yes to Alice at $0.65. Alice has paid $6.50 for 10 Yes, Bob's 10 No have cost him $3.50 net, and the market's vault holds $10.00. At 3:30 PM devnet halts, and it comes back at 6:00 PM. META closed at $684.00 at 4:00 PM.
- **What the plan already does (D19).** When devnet returns, the bot settles using the price published at 4:00 PM, so Yes wins. Alice redeems 10 Yes for $10.00, a gain of $3.50, and Bob's 10 No pay $0, a loss of $3.50. The result is correct, but it lands about 2 hours after the close instead of within 10 minutes. This works only because of Q3's rule, which checks that the price was published near the market's close time and not near "now". A 2-hour-old price would fail a plain staleness check.
- **If it had been a ledger reset instead,** the market, the vault and both positions would vanish. It was play money, but the only record of that Tuesday would be whatever was saved off the chain.
- **How the Tuesday shows up under each option below:** (a) a missed day; (b) a day left out of the count, with an evidence file; (c) "settled correctly, 2 h late"; (d) the same day shown working on a local chain.

**3. The options and their evidence.**
- **(a) Count it as a miss and show the cause.** This matches the spec's literal words, which allow no exception (F6.18). It is honest and costs score.
- **(b) Leave it out as force majeure, with an evidence file.** The file would hold a log of each job run (the intended time, the result, and the transaction ID or the error), health checks of the devnet server taken at the time of failure, and a snapshot of the status page. In favour: devnet is unreliable by design (F6.15). Against: the spec offers no carve-out (F6.18). The outage history comes from a third-party blog, and no official devnet record was found (F6.16, SECONDARY). That graders accept such a file is reasoning only (F6.17, UNCONFIRMED), and logs you write yourself can't be checked independently.
- **(c) Settle late when devnet returns, and report the time to settle separately from the 10-minute target.** D19 already builds the catch-up. The spec's 15-minute retry rule covers an oracle failure, not a chain failure (F6.18), so this extends it. It works for a halt and not for a reset.
- **(d) Show the lost day on a second environment,** a local chain such as Surfpool (F6.07, SECONDARY). This proves the code works, but not that the system stayed live on devnet, and the spec requires devnet for a Solana project (the spec text quoted in the input file under A2).
- **Asking the graders.** Two steps are possible: state your counting rule up front in the README and the risks note (for example, "a trading day counts if devnet was healthy, per the evidence log"), and ask Gauntlet or Peak6 staff before the deadline. Who grades, and with what rubric, was not found (F6.18).
- **The plan's current proposal:** document a known limitation, log any missed day with evidence, and ask Peak6. That reports the day as in (a) or (b) and handles it as in (c).

**4. What each answer commits you to.**
- **(a):** you build nothing new beyond the logs, accept a visible miss, and say so in the risks note.
- **(b):** you build the evidence log and the health snapshots from the first day (Q53's backend-log layer covers most of this), you accept the risk that a grader rejects them, and the docs must ask for the exclusion, not claim it as a right.
- **(c):** you keep D19's catch-up and Q3's close-window rule, retries must keep going past the spec's 15 minutes while the chain is down, and the reports carry two numbers (on time, and late but correct). For a reset you also need a redeploy-from-scripts procedure and a dated incident note, which no item has yet.
- **(d):** you run and record a second environment, which means more infrastructure, and it still doesn't replace devnet.
- **Asking Peak6:** one message now. The answer may come late or never, so choose a default rule anyway.

**Recommendation (Claude's; yours to accept or reject).** These options can be combined. Keep (c) as the system's behaviour. Report a lost day honestly as late or missed, with its evidence attached. Ask Peak6 early, and state your counting rule up front rather than claiming an exclusion. Add a plan for a ledger reset, which no current item covers. Also, don't quote "devnet halted 3 times in 2026" in the docs until it has a source (see Conflicts, item 7).

### Q24. How does a grader see the full cycle in the evening or at a weekend?

**Your direction (decided 2026-10-07):** the devnet lifecycle script checks the market calendar. When the market is closed, it prints "Market closed; next window Monday 9:30 AM ET" and offers "Run the full lifecycle locally now (fake clock)? [y/N]". This briefing checks that direction against the evidence and sets out what is still open.

**1. The terms.**
- **Lifecycle:** create the market, then mint (lock $1.00 to get one Yes plus one No), trade on the order book, settle and redeem.
- **Oracle:** a service that publishes real-world prices onto the blockchain in a form a program can verify. The plan uses Pyth.
- **Staleness and confidence checks:** settle refuses a price that is too old or whose uncertainty band (Pyth's "± conf") is too wide. The spec requires both (F6.06).
- **Market sessions:** Pyth's US stock prices come in a regular session (9:30 AM to 4:00 PM ET), pre-market (4:00 to 9:30 AM), post-market (4:00 to 8:00 PM) and overnight, Sunday to Thursday 8 PM to 4 AM. Nothing is published from Friday 8 PM to Sunday 8 PM (F6.05). The page doesn't say what price, if any, is shown when a session is closed.
- **Fake clock and mock price:** on a local test chain, the test sets the time and writes the price account itself.
- **Transaction signature:** the unique ID of a transaction. Anyone can look it up on a block explorer, a website that shows what is on the chain.

**2. Worked example.** A grader runs the devnet script at 2:00 PM ET on a Saturday.
- **Under your direction,** the script stops at the calendar check and offers the local run. Locally, Bob mints 10 pairs for $10.00 of test USDC and sells the 10 Yes to Alice at $0.65: Alice pays $6.50, and Bob's 10 No have cost him $3.50 net. The test moves the clock to 4:05 PM and writes a fake Pyth price for META of $684.20 ± $0.10, published at 4:00:00. The whole band (from $684.10 to $684.30) is above $680, so Yes wins. Alice redeems 10 Yes for $10.00, a gain of $3.50. Bob's No are worth $0, a loss of $3.50, and the vault goes from $10.00 to $0. The program and the checks are the same as on devnet, but the run happens on the grader's machine.
- **On a Tuesday at 6:00 PM,** the script would also stop under your rule. Post-market prices exist until 8:00 PM (F6.05), so in principle a demo market closing at 6:05 PM could settle on a 6:05 PM price. That price would not be a 4:00 PM close, though, and the market would need a different, extended-hours price feed.

**3. The options and their evidence.**
1. **Devnet only in market hours, the local fake-clock run at other times (your direction).** Supported: local tools can set the price and the clock (F6.07, SECONDARY), and the checks stay intact (F6.06). Gap: the spec says the demo must be "reproducible on testnet" (F6.06), and the local run doesn't happen on testnet.
2. **An evening demo window using Pyth's extended sessions** (F6.05, CONFIRMED). This covers weekday evenings until 8 PM and the Sunday-to-Thursday overnight session. It doesn't cover Friday night to Sunday evening, and the price is not a closing price. Whether these feeds exist on devnet is unknown (F6.19, SECONDARY). The plan's own research says extended hours are on a paid tier (plan, SECONDARY).
3. **A mock price on devnet,** meaning a price account our side writes. This is the report's leading answer for D11. Its own case against: it can look like faked settlement, and it widens the surface open to attack. It would also need a bypass of D28, which accepts only price accounts owned by Pyth's program. You cut this option.
4. **A recorded real devnet run.** The README lists the transaction signatures from a real weekday run, alongside the video already planned in Q55 point 5. Anyone can check them on an explorer at any hour. Risk: a devnet ledger reset would erase them (F6.15), so keep an archived copy.
5. **The admin-override path with a short delay, on demo markets only.** This is in the report's list, but no finding covers it. It shows the override step, not the oracle read, and the spec's delay is about an hour (D8).
6. **A local chain with copies of real accounts.** Surfpool can copy account state from a live network (F6.07, SECONDARY). It is closer to real data, but still local.

**4. What each answer commits you to.**
1. You build the calendar check and the prompt. The local lifecycle run must be clean enough to serve as a demo, not only as a test. You accept that a weekend grader can't reproduce the cycle on testnet.
2. The Q16 spike must confirm that the trial key includes extended-hours feeds that verify on devnet. You need a second feed ID per stock for demo markets, and the docs must say the demo price isn't a close. It still leaves about 48 hours each weekend with no prices.
3. You reverse D11, keep a code path that trusts a price not signed by Pyth, and take on more to test and explain.
4. You record one run in market hours (the video is already planned), add its signatures and archive them. This is cheap.
5. You add a short delay used only for demos, which is a second code path to build and explain.
6. The same as option 1, plus the setup to copy accounts.

**Recommendation (Claude's; yours to accept or reject).** Keep your direction and add option 4: put the signatures from a real devnet run in the README, with an archived copy. Consider option 2 only if the Q16 spike shows that the trial key comes with extended-hours feeds on devnet. Also reword the plan's "stock prices only update 9:30 to 4:00" to "the regular-session feed only updates 9:30 to 4:00".

### Q55. Can a grader set it up with one command on Windows, macOS and Linux?

**1. The terms.**
- **One-command setup:** the spec asks for a README with "one-command setup (e.g., make dev or equivalent)", plus scripts to deploy, create markets and run the lifecycle (F6.23). `make` is a build tool that runs a named list of steps. It isn't built into Windows.
- **WSL** (Windows Subsystem for Linux) is a Linux environment inside Windows. Anchor's and Solana's docs say Windows development must go through it (F6.21, SECONDARY).
- **The toolchain:** Rust, the Solana command-line tools (version 3.x, with 3.1.10 recommended), Anchor, Surfpool version 1.1.2 or later, and Node (F6.01, F6.22).
- **API key:** a password-like string a service issues to identify who is calling. Pyth's price service, Hermes, now requires one (F6.20). This review found no word on a free tier.
- **Devnet SOL:** SOL is Solana's own coin. On devnet it is free play coin from a **faucet** (a website or command that hands out test coins, with limits). Every transaction pays a small fee in SOL, and each new account needs a SOL deposit called rent.
- **Rate limit:** the public devnet server accepts at most 100 requests per 10 seconds from one internet address (F6.15, F6.23).

**2. Worked example.** A grader on a Windows laptop.
- **The local tier, `make dev`, needs no keys.** Inside WSL, the command installs the toolchain, starts a local chain, deploys the program, and starts the bot and the website with fake prices. In the browser, Bob clicks Buy No on "META ≥ $680 today?". The program mints 10 pairs with $10.00 of local test USDC and sells the 10 Yes to Alice at $0.65, so Bob's No cost him $3.50 and Alice's Yes cost her $6.50. With a fake 4:00 PM price of $684.20, Alice redeems for $10.00. This run needs no key and no devnet SOL, and no internet access beyond the installs.
- **The devnet tier** runs the same cycle on devnet. It needs a Pyth key (F6.20), devnet SOL for fees and rent, and test USDC, and it only finishes during market hours (Q24).

**3. The options and their evidence.**
- **A. One native command on all three systems.** The evidence doesn't support this: no native Windows version of the Solana 3.x tools was found, and the docs send Windows users to WSL (F6.21, SECONDARY).
- **B. `make dev`, plus an equivalent npm script, run inside WSL on Windows and directly on macOS and Linux.** This is the plan's proposal, and it matches F6.21 and F6.22. Version pinning matters (F6.22), and the first build is long.
- **C. A container** (Docker or a development container) that ships with the toolchain already installed. This removes most install trouble, but graders would need Docker, and no finding says whether they would accept it.
- **D. Two tiers:** a local command with no keys, and a separate devnet command that needs keys. The plan already has this shape (Q55 point 2 plus Q24). It takes the key and the SOL off the path of the "one command".
- **Who supplies the Pyth key for the devnet tier:** (i) graders get their own Pyth trial key; (ii) graders use our hosted backend, which holds our key; or (iii) the fallback, which reads Pyth prices someone else posts on devnet. F6.20 confirms that a key is needed. Never put our key in the repo (D17).
- **Devnet SOL:** document the faucet, or point graders at our deployed copy.

**4. What each answer commits you to.**
- **A:** this isn't available, so don't promise it.
- **B:** you write the install script, test it on all three systems and pin the versions. WSL becomes a documented Windows step.
- **C:** you maintain a container image, and graders need Docker.
- **D:** the README has two paths and two commands, and it must say what each proves: the local run proves the code, and the devnet run proves the live system.
- **Key (i):** graders sign up with Pyth, and the trial length is unknown (plan, Q16).
- **Key (ii):** our backend must be running during grading, and the grader is then using our system rather than reproducing it, which is weaker against the spec's "reproducible scripts" (F6.23).
- **Key (iii):** you depend on someone else's posting schedule and licence, which the plan hasn't verified (Q16).
- **Devnet SOL:** the faucet limits apply, and deploying from scratch costs more SOL than checking an existing deployment.

**Recommendation (Claude's; yours to accept or reject).** Use D with B, which is the plan's shape, and make the no-key local command the "one command" in the README. Offer a container only if Phase 0 shows that setting up WSL is painful. The report also flags that this repo sits in a OneDrive-synced folder, which can slow or break builds. That isn't a finding, but it is worth a check in Phase 0.

## Conflicts

1. **"Simulation mode": D11, A2, Q24 and Q55.** D11 cuts a simulation mode, yet A2 lists "a local or simulation demo" as a safeguard. Q55 runs `make dev` with "fake price records for local development only", and Q24 offers the local fake-clock test. The report's leading answer for D11 recommends a demo mode, while its own case against says a mock price is open to attack. The findings support a local fake price with the checks unchanged (F6.06, F6.07), which Q24 and Q55 already provide. So the conflict is mostly wording, plus one rule to write down: the deployed program has no bypass.
2. **HyperLiquid: D4 against D18.** D4 says "not explored (one-line note)", while D18 documents HIP-4 and the other alternatives. HIP-4 exists (F6.03, CONFIRMED), so a one-liner that repeats the spec's "may not natively support" would be wrong. D4's note should point to D18.
3. **Next.js: D4 against Q26.** D4 names Next.js, and Q26 defers the choice between Next.js and plain React to Architecture. The spec only recommends Next.js, and either choice needs a written reason (F6.02).
4. **HIP-4 facts: the plan's research against this report.** The plan (7 October) cites HyperLiquid's deployer docs as testnet-only with 10 active markets per deployer (CONFIRMED in the plan). It treats third-party deployment on mainnet since 29 August and a 500,000 HYPE bond as UNCONFIRMED. This report found that the official HIP-4 page mentions only an "initial mainnet release" and no testnet (F6.03, CONFIRMED). News reports say HIP-4 has been live on mainnet since 2 May 2026, is curated, settles in USDH, and plans open deployment at 500,000 HYPE (F6.04, SECONDARY), and those reports disagree with each other on details. Both sources agree that HIP-4 doesn't fit Meridian. The dates and deployment rules need one fresh check before D18 is written up.
5. **Finality times: the report against the plan.** In its case against D18, the report says Solana takes about 12.8 seconds to finalize, a figure it did not verify (F6.11, UNCONFIRMED). The plan measured mainnet at 31 to 33 slots of about 0.27 seconds each, roughly 8 to 9 seconds, and devnet at under a second (Q12, from a direct check of the chain). The plan's AI_USAGE item 9 already corrected the 13-second figure. The report's number carries no weight; keep it out of the docs.
6. **When Pyth publishes stock prices: Q24 and D11 against F6.05.** The plan says stock prices only update from 9:30 to 4:00 ET. F6.05 (CONFIRMED) shows that Pyth also publishes pre-market, post-market and Sunday-to-Thursday overnight sessions. The plan's own research says the regular-session feed stops at 4:00 PM (CONFIRMED) and that extended hours are on the paid Pro tier (SECONDARY). Both can be true if the plan's sentence is narrowed to the regular-session feed. The extended sessions also open an evening demo window (Q24, option 2) that hasn't been weighed yet.
7. **The devnet outage record: the plan against the report.** The plan says devnet halted or restarted 3 times in 2026, on 16 January, 14 to 16 April and 19 June (SECONDARY). This report found no official devnet record, only a mention of a devnet outage in February 2024 (F6.16, SECONDARY after being downgraded). So the claim is not corroborated. Also, 19 June 2026 is a market holiday in the plan's own calendar, so at most two of those dates fell on trading days.
8. **Mollusk and the clock: the plan against F6.12.** The plan's research says Mollusk 0.16.0 can set the clock and insert fake price accounts (CONFIRMED in the plan). F6.12 (SECONDARY) says Mollusk supports neither system accounts such as the clock nor calls into other programs, but its source is a guide for a competing tool. The plan's label is stronger, though it wasn't checked again here. LiteSVM is the main tool either way, so test this in the first milestone.
9. **Halts against resets: A2 and D19 against F6.15.** D19 plans for a halt ("a halt keeps all state, unlike a ledger reset"), and A2's catch-up assumes the market still exists afterwards. Devnet may be reset (F6.15, CONFIRMED). A reset would also erase the transaction signatures that Q24's recorded run and Q23's evidence would point to, so archive them off the chain.
