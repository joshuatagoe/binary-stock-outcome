## Summary

This group covers what a grader can check about Meridian: the languages and tools (D4), the blockchain (D18), the tests (D30), whether to add a "simulation mode" that fakes prices and time (D11), and how the project copes with Solana's **devnet**, the free public test network where all money is play money (A2, Q23, Q24, Q55). Four of the five decisions hold with a caveat. The stack is the spec's preferred one and is current (F6.04, F6.05). However, D4's "HyperLiquid: not explored" note is out of date, because HyperLiquid now runs its own yes/no "outcome markets", called HIP-4 (F6.06, F6.07). The chain choice holds on its strongest reason: the spec wants the market functions inside the project's own program (F6.27), and HyperLiquid's and Injective's built-in markets can't give that (F6.07, F6.25). Sui is the exception. Its built-in order book can be called from your own program (F6.23), so the write-up needs a different reason for passing on Sui. The write-up must also state **finality** (the moment a transaction can no longer be reversed) for devnet, which switched to Solana's new Alpenglow consensus on 25 September (F6.20). D30's test plan points the right way. It should add a real devnet run, because local tests skip Pyth's real signature check (F6.39), plus tests for duplicate accounts (F6.40), compute limits and bot crashes. D11 ("no simulation mode") holds, although the report's own leading answer would add one. Real venues do keep a fake-money, fake-clock sandbox (F6.15, F6.16), but your local fake-clock run already is that sandbox in all but name, so the plan only needs to describe it one way. A2, your view that a devnet outage won't count against the project, is contested. The spec's **liveness** rule (markets created and settled on time "every trading day") has no exception (F6.48), and Solana publishes no devnet outage record you could point to (F6.29). Your Q24 direction holds with a caveat: a weekend grader can't reproduce the cycle on devnet itself, and signatures from a recorded devnet run would close most of that gap. Q23 and Q55 are your call. The weakest evidence is what this run could not find: the graders' rubric, any devnet outage history, how Pyth's stock prices behave on devnet after hours, and a timed one-command setup on a clean Windows machine.

## Briefings

### Q23. How does a day lost to a devnet outage count?

**1. The terms.**
- **Liveness** is the spec's measure that the system does its daily job: "Markets created before market open and settled within 10 minutes of market close every trading day" (F6.48). To **settle** is to record which side won, using the stock's closing price from the **oracle** (the service that brings real-world prices onto the blockchain in a form a program can check). To **redeem** is to swap a winning token for $1.00.
- A **trading day** is a weekday when the NYSE is open. In 2026 it is shut on ten holidays and closes early, at 1:00 PM ET, on 27 November and 24 December (F6.37).
- In a **devnet halt**, the network stops making blocks for a while, and everything on it is frozen but kept. In a **ledger reset**, the network is wiped and restarted from empty, so programs, markets and balances on it are gone. Solana's docs say devnet "may be subject to ledger resets" and give no uptime figure (F6.01).
- An **RPC endpoint** is the server your software talks to in order to reach the chain. The public devnet one is a single shared server, and it can refuse requests with "429" (too many requests) or "403" (blocked) (F6.01, F6.46).
- Evidence tools: **LastRestartSlot** is a value the chain keeps itself, recording when the network was last restarted (F6.42). The **genesis hash** is the fingerprint of a chain's first block, so a new one means a new chain, as after a reset (F6.43). **OpenTimestamps** is a free service that proves a file existed before a given time, anchored to Bitcoin. It proves when, not whether the content is true (F6.44).

**2. Worked example.** On a Tuesday, Bob clicks Buy No on "META ≥ $680 today?". The program locks $10.00 of test USDC to mint 10 Yes and 10 No, and sells the 10 Yes to Alice at $0.65. Alice has paid $6.50 for 10 Yes, Bob's 10 No have cost him $10.00 − $6.50 = $3.50, and the market's vault holds $10.00. At 3:30 PM devnet halts, and it comes back at 6:00 PM. META closed at $684.00.
- **What the plan already does (D19).** When devnet returns, the bot settles with the price published at 4:00 PM, so Yes wins. Alice redeems for $10.00, a gain of $3.50, and Bob's No pay $0, a loss of $3.50. The result is right, but it lands about two hours after the close instead of within 10 minutes. This only works because of Q3's rule, which checks that the price was published near the market's close. Pyth's standard check measures the price's age against the chain's current clock (F6.14), so a 2-hour-old price would fail it.
- **On the scorecard,** over a 20-trading-day month with that one Tuesday: counted as a miss, liveness is 19 of 20 days, or 95%. Left out with evidence, it is 19 of 19, or 100%.
- **If it had been a ledger reset,** the market, the vault and both positions would vanish. It was play money, but the only record of that Tuesday would be what was saved off the chain.

**3. The options and their evidence.** These can be combined.
- **(A) Count it as a miss and report the raw rate.** This matches the spec's words, which have no exception for chain outages (F6.48). It is simple and honest, and it costs score for a cause you don't control.
- **(B) Report two numbers: the raw rate, and an adjusted rate that leaves out days with evidence.** The evidence would be a "heartbeat" log showing devnet's block counter not moving, or a changed genesis hash or LastRestartSlot (F6.42, F6.43). In favour: there is no official devnet record (F6.29), so the proof has to be yours. Against: you write the log yourself. A log from a single server can't tell a devnet outage from a failure of your server or the shared public one (F6.46), unless you check more than one. The idea that graders would accept such a file is reasoning only (F6.47, UNCONFIRMED). OpenTimestamps can show the log existed before grading, but not that it is true (F6.44).
- **(C) Replay the lost day on a local chain such as Surfpool** (F6.18). This shows the service would have met the target. It doesn't show that devnet was up.
- **(D) Define "trading day" from the NYSE calendar** (F6.37), so holidays never count and early-close days settle at about 1:05 PM (the plan's D12 and Q14). The spec fixes 4:00 PM and says nothing about early closes. This doesn't solve outages, but it stops days that aren't trading days from being counted as misses.
- **(E) Ask the graders,** and state your counting rule in the README before any outage happens, so it can't look like an excuse made afterwards. No public statement on this was found (F6.50, UNCONFIRMED). Questions to ask: is the 10-minute metric measured on a clean run, are outage days left out, and does a late settle (by the bot or the admin override) count.
- **A related risk:** if the automation ran on a scheduler that can delay or drop runs, such as GitHub Actions (F6.38), a missed day would be your fault, not devnet's. The plan's always-on service (D23) avoids this.

**Your related view (A2)** is that an outage won't be held against you. This review rates it **Contested**: the spec has no exception (F6.48), and nothing found says graders will excuse a day. The **plan's proposal** (document it as a known limitation, log any missed day with evidence, ask Peak6) fits the evidence, apart from the uncorroborated "3 times in 2026".

**4. What each answer commits you to.**
- **(A):** nothing new beyond ordinary logs. A visible miss, explained in the risks note.
- **(B):** a heartbeat job from day one that records the block counter, health, genesis hash and LastRestartSlot from at least two RPC endpoints. The log is saved daily, optionally with OpenTimestamps. The docs ask for the exclusion rather than claim it, and you accept that a grader may reject it.
- **(C):** a replay script and its saved output. This is more infrastructure, and it still doesn't replace devnet.
- **(D):** the calendar table you already plan (D12) with its unit test, and settlement at about 1:05 PM on early-close days.
- **(E):** one message now. You still need a default rule, because the answer may come late or never.
- **For any of them:** a plan for a ledger reset, which no current item has. The report suggests deploy scripts that are safe to run twice and reuse the same program address (A2's leading answer).

**Recommendation (Claude's; yours to accept or reject).** Combine D, E and B. Let the calendar define the days. State the counting rule in the README before the first graded day. Report both numbers with the evidence attached, and ask Peak6 now. Add a ledger-reset procedure, and drop "devnet halted 3 times in 2026" until it has a source.

### Q24. How does a grader see the full cycle in the evening or at a weekend?

**Your direction (decided 2026-10-07):** the devnet lifecycle script checks the market calendar first. When the market is closed, it prints "Market closed; next window Monday 9:30 AM ET" and offers "Run the full lifecycle locally now (fake clock)? [y/N]", which runs the local lifecycle test. **Verdict on the direction: Holds with a caveat** (see the table and below).

**1. The terms.**
- **Lifecycle:** create the market, mint (lock $1.00 to get one Yes plus one No), trade on the order book, settle, redeem.
- **Pyth** is the oracle the plan uses. **Hermes** is Pyth's service that hands out signed price updates. Since Pyth's upgrade finished on 26 August 2026, every Hermes user needs an **API key** (a password-like string that identifies the caller). There is a free trial, then paid plans, and no prices are given on the page (F6.12).
- **Staleness and confidence checks:** settle refuses a price that is too old, or whose uncertainty band ("± conf") is too wide.
- **Market sessions:** Pyth lists US stocks in a regular session (9:30 AM to 4:00 PM ET on weekdays), pre-market (4:00 to 9:30 AM), post-market (4:00 to 8:00 PM) and an overnight session from Sunday to Thursday (8:00 PM to 4:00 AM). It is closed at weekends and on NYSE holidays (F6.10). Pyth's own advice is that outside trading hours "it's not clear what an equity's price is" (F6.11).
- **Fake clock and mock price:** in LiteSVM, the test sets the time and writes the price account itself (F6.17). **Surfpool** is a local test chain with a "time travel" feature (F6.18).
- **Transaction signature:** the unique ID of a transaction, which anyone can look up on a **block explorer** (a website showing what is on the chain).

**2. Worked example: one trade, run by a grader at three different times.** Bob's Buy No mints 10 pairs for $10.00 and sells the 10 Yes to Alice at $0.65. Alice pays $6.50, and Bob's No cost him $3.50.
- **Saturday, 2:00 PM ET.** The script stops at the calendar check, and the grader types "y". Locally, the test moves the clock to 4:05 PM and writes a fake Pyth price for META of $684.20 ± $0.10, published at 4:00:00 PM. The whole band, from $684.10 to $684.30, is above $680, so Yes wins. Alice redeems for $10.00, a gain of $3.50, Bob loses $3.50, and the vault goes from $10.00 to $0. The program and its checks are the same as on devnet, but the run happens on the grader's machine, and Pyth's real signature check is skipped (F6.39).
- **Tuesday, 11:00 AM ET.** The script runs on devnet. It creates a demo market whose own close is 11:10 AM (D11). At 11:11 the bot uses the project's Pyth key to fetch the 11:10:00 price, which is $678.90 ± $0.08. The whole band is below $680, so No wins. Bob redeems 10 No for $10.00, a gain of $6.50, and Alice loses $6.50. Everything is on devnet and checkable on an explorer.
- **Tuesday, 9:00 PM ET.** Under your rule the script stops. A demo market closing at 9:05 PM would have no regular-session price. Only the overnight session publishes then (F6.10), and that price isn't a close.

**3. The options and their evidence.**
1. **Devnet only in market hours, with the local fake-clock run at other times (your direction),** plus a published schedule and a recorded run. Supported: local tools can set the clock and the price (F6.17, F6.18), and the deployed program keeps every check. The gap: the spec and S23 say "end-to-end on testnet" (F6.53).
2. **Use the extended sessions (post-market and overnight) for evening demos.** These cover weekday evenings and Sunday-to-Thursday nights, but not Friday 8 PM to Sunday 8 PM (F6.10), and the price isn't a closing price. Whether Pyth's feeds for these sessions exist for the seven stocks on devnet is not confirmed (report gap). The plan's own research says extended hours sit on the paid Pro tier (plan, SECONDARY).
3. **Settle later on a price stamped at the close.** Pyth's Solana docs say its price accounts suit apps that "consume prices for a specific timestamp" (F6.14). Hermes lists a "historical price at timestamp" endpoint, but only the title was seen (F6.52, UNCONFIRMED). This needs Q3's close-window rule and a key. Create, mint and trade must still happen before the market's close, so it only moves settle and redeem later.
4. **A mock price on devnet (a simulation mode),** like Kalshi's demo environment (F6.15). This is the report's lead for D11. It would need a way past D28's rule that settle accepts only price accounts owned by Pyth's program. You cut it.
5. **A 24-hour crypto price as a stand-in for a demo market.** No finding covers this. It would step outside the spec's seven stocks for the demo, and the config would have to allow a non-stock price feed.
6. **Surfpool with time travel and account overrides** for the off-hours run (F6.18). It is closer to a real chain than an in-process test, but still local, and its Windows support is undocumented (F6.18).

**Something to fix in the plan's wording.** Q24 says a grader at 9 PM "would get stuck at settle (staleness check)". That is true of a market closing in the evening. A market that closed at 4:00 PM can still be settled at 9 PM under Q3's close-window rule, and D19 relies on exactly that. The plan should state one rule (Conflict 11).

**4. What each answer commits you to.**
1. You build the calendar check and the prompt, and make the local run clean enough to be a demo, not only a test. You accept the weekend gap. The recorded run costs one market-hours session plus archiving the signatures.
2. The Q16 spike must confirm extended-hours stock feeds that verify on devnet. Each stock needs a second feed ID, and the docs must say the demo price isn't a close. About 48 hours each weekend stay uncovered.
3. You write and test the close-window check (already in D30 as "publish-time window"), and keep or re-fetch the update for the close.
4. You reverse a decision you made (D11, Q16), add a code path that trusts a price Pyth didn't sign, and take on more to test and explain.
5. You add a non-stock feed to the config, and possibly a different Pyth plan.
6. You set up Surfpool in the local tier, which Q55 already proposes for `make dev`.

**Recommendation (Claude's; yours to accept or reject).** Keep your direction and add option 1's recorded run: put the transaction signatures from a real weekday devnet run in the README, with an archived copy off the chain, next to the demo video already planned in Q55. Have the local prompt say plainly that the local run doesn't check Pyth's signatures. Reword "stock prices only update 9:30 to 4:00" to "the regular-session feed only updates 9:30 to 4:00". Weigh option 2 only if the Q16 spike shows extended-hours feeds on devnet.

### Q55. Can a grader set it up with one command on Windows, macOS and Linux?

**1. The terms.**
- **One-command setup:** the spec asks for a "Clear README with one-command setup (e.g., make dev or equivalent)", plus reproducible scripts to deploy, create markets and run the lifecycle, secrets kept in environment variables with a `.env.example` file, and tests that run locally (F6.58).
- **make** is a build tool that runs a named list of steps. It is generally not installed on Windows by default (F6.59, UNCONFIRMED: general knowledge, no source found).
- **WSL** (Windows Subsystem for Linux) is a Linux environment inside Windows. Anchor's install page says Windows users must install it first (F6.56).
- **The toolchain:** Solana's installer sets up Rust, the Solana command-line tools, Anchor, Surfpool, Node and Yarn (F6.30). `anchor test` now starts Surfpool by default, version 1.1.2 or later (F6.62).
- **Docker** packages a ready-made machine image with the tools already inside, so the grader installs Docker instead of the tools.
- **Devnet SOL:** SOL is Solana's own coin. On devnet it is free play coin from a **faucet**, a website or command that hands out test coins. Every transaction pays a small SOL fee, and each new account needs a SOL deposit. Solana's faucet allows 2 requests every 8 hours and asks AI agents not to use it (F6.57).
- **Rate limit:** the public devnet server refuses callers who send too much ("429") (F6.46).

**2. Worked example: a grader on a Windows 11 laptop.**
- **Step 0.** `wsl --install` needs admin rights and possibly a restart (the report's reading of Solana's docs).
- **`make dev` inside Ubuntu (the local tier).** It installs the toolchain (F6.30), starts Surfpool, builds and deploys the program, and starts the bot and the website with fake price records. In the browser, Bob clicks Buy No on "META ≥ $680 today?". The program mints 10 pairs with $10.00 of local test USDC and sells the 10 Yes to Alice at $0.65, so Alice pays $6.50 and Bob's No cost him $3.50. With a fake 4:00 PM price of $684.20, Yes wins and Alice redeems for $10.00. This tier needs no key, no devnet SOL and no faucet.
- **The devnet tier.** The same cycle on devnet needs a Pyth key in `.env` (F6.12), devnet SOL for fees and deposits from the rate-limited faucet (F6.57), and test USDC. It finishes only in market hours (Q24).
- **What could block the grader:** no admin rights for WSL or Docker; a long first download (not measured); an expired or missing Pyth trial key; the faucet limit; "429" errors from the public server (F6.46); a devnet reset (F6.01).

**3. The options and their evidence.**
- **(1) Docker or a dev container.** Surfpool publishes a Docker image (F6.60). Whether the whole toolchain works in one container on Windows and macOS was not tested (F6.60), and a community post runs Solana in Docker on Windows without WSL (F6.55, SECONDARY). It needs Docker Desktop on Windows and macOS.
- **(2) Native scripts, with WSL documented for Windows (the plan's proposal).** Anchor tells Windows users to use WSL (F6.56). A native Windows install is uncertain: Solana's and Anza's pages contradict each other (F6.54, UNCONFIRMED), and an open issue reports the old local validator misbehaving on Windows (F6.55, SECONDARY). Surfpool doesn't mention Windows (F6.18).
- **(3) Two tiers:** `make dev` runs fully locally with no keys, and a separate `make devnet` needs the key and SOL. The plan already has this shape (Q55 point 2, plus Q24). It takes the three outside blockers off the first command: the key (F6.12), the faucet (F6.57) and the public server's rate limit (F6.46). That it removes "near all" blockers is reasoning (F6.61, UNCONFIRMED). It also matches the spec's "tests run locally" (F6.58).
- **(4) An npm script, or another task runner, next to or instead of make,** because make isn't standard on Windows (F6.59, UNCONFIRMED). The plan proposes both.
- **Who supplies the Pyth key for the devnet tier:** (i) graders get their own trial key (F6.12); (ii) graders use our hosted backend, which holds our key; or (iii) the fallback in Q16, reading Pyth prices someone else posts on devnet (Pyth's on-chain receiver is on devnet, F6.14). Never commit our key (D17).
- **Devnet SOL:** document the faucet and its alternatives (command-line airdrop, a proof-of-work faucet, a local validator, F6.57), or point graders at our deployed copy.

**4. What each answer commits you to.**
