## Terms used in the table

- **Program:** the code that runs on the Solana blockchain (often called a smart contract). A **function** (Solana says "instruction") is one action it offers, such as Mint Pair.
- **Devnet:** Solana's public test network, where all money is play money. **Mainnet** is the real network.
- **Key:** the secret that signs (approves) transactions for an account. Whoever holds it can act as that account. The plan has three: the **admin key** (pause, override, config), the **bot key** (the backend's wallet for the daily jobs; the report calls it the automation wallet or operator) and the **upgrade key** (allowed to replace the program's code).
- **Environment variable:** a named setting that the computer hands to a running service from outside its code. A `.env` file holds them on one machine and is never committed to git. A `.env.example` file is committed: it lists the names with dummy values.
- **Oracle:** the service that brings real-world prices on-chain in a form the program can verify. The plan uses **Pyth**. **Hermes** is Pyth's web service that hands out signed price updates, and since 26 August 2026 it requires an **API key**, a password for the service (F5.04).
- **Backend:** the always-on service in our repo that runs the 8:00, 8:30 and 4:05 jobs and streams live data to browsers.
- **Vault:** the program's account that holds the locked USDC, $1.00 per Yes+No pair.
- **Mint authority:** the one account allowed to create new tokens. **Freeze authority:** the one account allowed to lock a holder's token account.
- **Multisig:** a wallet that needs, say, 2 of 3 named people to sign. **Squads** is the common Solana multisig.
- **Halt:** devnet stops making blocks but keeps everything. **Reset:** devnet is wiped and restarted empty.
- **Settle:** record which side won. **Redeem:** swap a winning token for $1.00.
- **Order book:** the list of open buy and sell offers. A **resting order** is an offer waiting on the book.
- **Rent:** a deposit in SOL (Solana's own coin, used for fees) that keeps an account stored on-chain. It is returned when the account is closed.

## Briefings

The running example throughout: "META ≥ $680 today?", Yes at $0.65, No at $0.35, 10 contracts. Bob clicks Buy No: the program locks $10.00 of his USDC in the vault, makes 10 Yes plus 10 No, and sells the 10 Yes to Alice at $0.65. Alice pays $6.50. Bob's net cost for 10 No is $3.50. If META closes at $684.00, Yes wins: Alice redeems 10 Yes for $10.00 (a $3.50 gain) and Bob's No pay $0 (a $3.50 loss).

### Q12. Which confirmation level counts as "sub-second finality"?

**Your direction (2026-10-07):** mainnet's lag doesn't matter for a devnet submission, and Alpenglow will reach mainnet eventually. On devnet, wait for "finalized". The two-stage screen ("Filled · confirming", then "Final") is deferred to the mainnet bonus. **Verdict: Holds with a caveat.** It rests on devnet already running Alpenglow, and the evidence now disagrees about that (Conflict 1).

**1. The terms.**
- A **slot** is Solana's short time step, in which one validator (one of the computers that run the network) may add a block of transactions. Slots used to be 400 ms. Reports say mainnet reached 200 ms on 9 October 2026 (F5.46, SECONDARY), and the plan measured devnet blocks at about 0.24 s.
- A **commitment level** says how settled a transaction is (F5.42, CONFIRMED). **Processed:** the newest view, which "can still be rolled back". **Confirmed:** more than two-thirds of the network's stake has voted for the block. **Finalized:** the network has committed to the block with "maximum lockout", meaning the votes behind it can no longer move.
- **Finality** is the moment a transaction can't be reversed. Strictly, only "finalized" is finality.
- **TowerBFT** is Solana's current voting method. Under it, "finalized" trails "confirmed" by about 32 slots: about 12.8 s at 400 ms slots (F5.44). **Alpenglow** is its replacement, with a target of about 150 ms to finality, which Solana calls "an expectation, not a constant" (F5.44).

**2. Worked example.** At 10:00:00 Alice's Buy Yes for 10 at up to $0.66 meets Bob's offer at $0.65, and she pays $6.50.
- About 0.4 s later the trade is **processed**. If the screen said "Filled" now and the block were dropped, Alice would see a $6.50 trade that never happened.
- About 0.6 s later it is **confirmed** (F5.43, SECONDARY). Helius, a Solana data provider, says no confirmed block has ever been reversed (F5.43, SECONDARY, not checked here).
- When it is **finalized** depends on the voting method. Under Alpenglow, finality comes about 150 ms after the transaction lands (F5.44), so by this point it would already be final. Under TowerBFT the wait is about 32 slots: 12.8 s at 400 ms (F5.44), 8 to 9 s at the 0.27 s slots the plan measured on mainnet on 7 October, and about 6.4 s at 200 ms (computed from F5.46).
- At 4:05 the bot settles: META closed at $684.00, so Yes wins. Alice's redeem pays out $10.00. Waiting even 9 s for "finalized" before paying her is nothing against the 4:10 target. The level matters for how fast the **trade** screen says "done", not for settlement.

**3. The options and their evidence.**
- **(A) Processed counts.** About 0.4 s, but it can be rolled back (F5.42), so it is not finality under any reading.
- **(B) Confirmed counts.** About 0.6 s (F5.43, SECONDARY). The report says this is what people usually mean by Solana's "sub-second", but no finding sources that. By the docs' own definitions it is not finality (F5.42).
- **(C) Finalized counts (the strict reading).** Mainnet on TowerBFT fails it: about 12.8 s per Solana's own page (F5.44), or 6 to 9 s at today's shorter slots. Devnet passes only if Alpenglow is active there. On 7 October the plan's direct check saw devnet's confirmed and finalized slots equal, while mainnet's were 31 to 33 slots apart. Solana's Alpenglow page still lists devnet activation as "planned" (F5.44, CONFIRMED), and a news report says Alpenglow runs only on testnet so far (F5.45, SECONDARY). Two cheap checks settle it: the getAgGenesisCert call, where an empty answer means a cluster still runs TowerBFT (F5.44), and the report's recipe of sending a small transaction 50 or more times and logging when each status arrives.
- **(D) Two-stage screen (the plan's deferred idea).** Show "Filled · confirming" at confirmed and "Final" at finalized. Settlement and redeem act only on finalized.

**4. What each answer commits you to.**
- **(A):** a screen that can show fills that later vanish. Not recommended by any source.
- **(B):** the docs say "confirmed in under a second, final in N seconds, measured on date X". The trade screen shows "Filled" at confirmed, and payouts wait for finalized. This is honest on both networks.
- **(C):** the "sub-second" claim holds only on a network running Alpenglow, so it rests on the devnet check above. If devnet turns out to be on TowerBFT, every trade waits several seconds before the screen says done, which works against the spec's "real-time" goal.
- **(D):** two states per trade to build and test. If devnet is not on Alpenglow, this moves from the bonus into the core.

**Recommendation (Claude's; yours to accept or reject).** Run getAgGenesisCert on devnet and mainnet, and the 50-sample timing, before writing the chain rationale (D18). Write finality as a dated measurement rather than a fixed number, since the slot length changed this very week (F5.46). If devnet is on Alpenglow, your direction stands as it is. If not, adopt (D) for the core.

### Q25. Who can upgrade the program, and how should the upgrade key be held?

No direction from you yet. **Verdict: Your call.**

**1. The terms.**
- The **upgrade authority** (the upgrade key) is "the account that has permission to update or close the program". By default it is the wallet that deployed the program (F5.47, CONFIRMED).
- A **buffer** is a staging account where a new version of the program is uploaded before it replaces the old one. Squads upgrades through one (F5.48).
- A **time lock** delays a transaction that has already been approved, for example by 1 hour, 1 day or 1 week, so others can see it coming (F5.49).
- **Immutable** (`set-upgrade-authority --final`): "Once a program is immutable, it can never be updated or closed" (F5.47).
- A **verified build** proves that the code on-chain matches the public source code. Solana's docs say it is "not more secure than an unverified one": it proves a match, not safety (F5.51).

**2. Worked example.** At 4:05 the market settles Yes (META closed at $684.00). The vault holds $10.00. Alice's 10 Yes are worth $10.00 and Bob's 10 No are worth $0. Alice plans to redeem in the morning. That evening, the laptop holding the upgrade key is stolen.
- **One plain key (option 1):** the thief can deploy new code at once, for example code that sends the vault's $10.00 to the thief, or that rewrites the outcome to No so that No holders collect. Alice finds nothing to redeem in the morning. The spec's "outcome is immutable once written" was only ever as strong as this key (F5.47).
- **A 2-of-3 Squads multisig with a 1-day time lock (option 2):** one stolen laptop is not enough. Even if two signers are compromised, the upgrade sits in public view for a day, and Alice, or anyone watching, can redeem the $10.00 first, because redeem can never be paused (Q44). Whether Squads' time lock applies to upgrades has not been tested (F5.49).
- **Immutable (option 4):** nobody can change the code, so the thief gets nothing. But if a bug found tomorrow stops redeem, Alice's $10.00 is stuck forever, because nobody can fix it either.

**3. The options and their evidence.**
- **(1) A single deployer key.** This is the default (F5.47). It is the simplest, and during development and after a devnet reset you need to redeploy freely. It is a single point of failure.
- **(2) Move the key into a Squads multisig, optionally with a time lock.** Squads documents handing a program's upgrade authority to the multisig and upgrading through a buffer that the members approve, with a "Safe Authority Transfer" and a GitHub Action for CI (F5.48). Squads is audited by four firms (F5.50). Its own warnings: if the account that controls the multisig's settings is compromised, an attacker can change the members, and very long time locks can trap you (F5.49, F5.50). Its programs page does not mention time locks (F5.48), and its developer reference does not say whether upgrades are covered (F5.49). Whether Squads runs on devnet is unverified in the plan and was not checked here.
- **(3) A two-step handover through Pyth's escrow program.** Propose the new key, then accept it or take it back, so a typo'd destination can be undone. The program states no audit (F5.52).
- **(4) Make the program immutable.** This is permanent: no bug fixes, and rent can't be reclaimed (F5.47). The verified-build record must be created first, because only the upgrade key can create it; an immutable program without one needs OtterSec's help (F5.51).
- **(5) A hybrid.** Keep it upgradeable and held by a multisig during the pilot, then make it immutable after an audit and a stable period.
- **The plan's proposal:** one dedicated devnet upgrade key, kept apart from the admin key and the bot key, with who holds it documented; a multisig with a time lock required before any mainnet bonus; and, optionally, immutability after the final demo, noting that this can't be undone.

**4. What each answer commits you to.**
- **(1):** a written statement that "immutable once written" means "no function in the program can rewrite an outcome; an upgrade could, and here is who holds that key". Keep the key off the server and out of D17's environment variables.
- **(2):** setting up and testing the multisig, testing that the time lock really delays upgrades (F5.49), and slower releases, because each one needs signers.
- **(3):** one more unaudited program in the path (F5.52).
- **(4):** no fixes ever again. On devnet, a reset wipes the program anyway (F5.11), so immutability there proves little.
- **(5):** the costs of (2) now and of (4) later, plus a written trigger for when to make the program immutable.

**Recommendation (Claude's; yours to accept or reject).** For the devnet submission, choose (1) with the plan's separation of keys, a hardware wallet or offline machine for the upgrade key, the written meaning of "immutable" above, and a published verified build. Leave (2) and the time-lock test to the mainnet bonus (Q49, deferred). Don't make the devnet program immutable: a reset can erase it, and a bug could then never be fixed.

### Q26. Next.js or plain React, built and hosted with no server?

**Your direction (2026-10-07):** a React website with no server of its own. Choosing between Next.js and plain React is deferred to Architecture. **Verdict: Holds with a caveat.** The no-server shape works with either option (F5.28, F5.54). The caveats are the backend dependency and the page addresses, described below.

**1. The terms.**
- A **static site** is a set of plain files (HTML for the page text, CSS for the look, JavaScript for behaviour) that any file host can serve. No code of ours runs on the host. A **CDN** is a network of file hosts close to users.
- **Next.js static export** (`output: 'export'`) turns a Next.js project into such files in an `out` folder (F5.28). **Vite** is a build tool that turns a plain React project into files in a `dist` folder (F5.54).
- A **single-page app (SPA)** is one HTML page whose JavaScript draws every screen. When someone opens a deep link such as `/trade/META-680` directly, the host must be told to serve that one page (F5.54).
- A **framework** such as Next.js adds page organisation and build rules on top of React. React's own docs recommend starting with one (F5.53).

**2. Worked example.** Alice opens Meridian. Her browser downloads the site's files from a static host, and none of our code runs there. The page then opens one live stream from our backend (SSE, D16), which sends "META $683.20 (Pyth), Yes bid $0.64 / ask $0.66". She clicks Buy Yes for 10 at up to $0.66, her wallet signs, and the order goes straight to Solana, where it fills at $0.65 for $6.50. The website's own host did nothing beyond serving files. Two details matter for the choice.
- **The price line comes from the backend.** The Hermes key can't sit in files that every visitor downloads (F5.04; F5.37 is UNCONFIRMED, though the logic is the plan's own Q17). If the backend is down, Alice sees "reconnecting" (D23).
- **Today's markets don't exist when the site is built.** Bob shares a link to today's META $680 market. The 45 or so markets are created at 8:30 AM, after any build. A Next.js static export can't use server rewrites (F5.28), and from general knowledge (not checked in this review) it pre-builds one page per address, so the link must carry the market as a query, such as `/trade?market=META-680-2026-10-09`, or the host must route unknown addresses to one page. A Vite SPA has the same need: the host must send every address to `index.html` (F5.54). Cloudflare Pages does that by default (F5.56).

**3. The options and their evidence.**
- **(A) Next.js as a static export.** The spec recommends Next.js. Solana's official Kit template is `kit/nextjs`, and no Vite template was named on the page opened (F5.55). React's docs recommend a framework, and all of those frameworks can produce a static site (F5.53). Limits: no Server Actions, cookies, rewrites, redirects, headers or default image optimisation (F5.28). Wallet code must run only in the browser, for example inside `useEffect` (F5.57). Cloudflare points full-stack Next.js to a different adapter and static exports to its static-site guide (F5.56).
- **(B) Plain React with Vite.** It builds to `dist` and deploys to GitHub Pages, Cloudflare Pages, Netlify or Vercel. `vite preview` is not a production server, and fallback routing for a single-page app must be set per host (F5.54). You pick your own router. React's docs present this as the exception (F5.53).
- **(C) Next.js with its own server.** Your direction rules this out, and nothing in the evidence argues for bringing it back. The backend already does the server-side work.

**4. What each answer commits you to.**
- **(A):** follow the spec's recommendation and the official template; keep within the static-export limits; design page addresses around markets created each day; and test in the first milestone that the wallet library works in a static export (the plan already lists this).
- **(B):** a written reason for departing from the spec's recommendation (the spec asks you to justify major dependencies); your own router; and host fallback settings.
- **Either:** the site depends on the backend for all live data, which D16 already accepts. Showing the order book when the backend is down would need a direct read from Solana (see Conflict 9).

**Recommendation (Claude's; yours to accept or reject).** Option (A) has the stronger evidence: the spec's wording, the official template (F5.55) and React's own advice (F5.53), at the cost of static-export limits that Meridian doesn't need. Decide the page-address scheme before choosing, because it is the one place the two options differ in practice.

### Q44. What does Pause stop?

**Your direction (2026-10-08):** nobody may freeze people's money. You also caught a gap: making Settle unpausable removed the emergency brake for a wrong oracle price. The plan's resulting proposal is that Pause stops only Mint and Place Order; Cancel, Close Pair and Redeem are never pausable; and a separate, time-limited **settlement hold** lets the admin or the bot's pre-check delay one market's settlement until a deadline (for example 24 hours after the close). During the hold, the result is set only through the two-step override (Q43). After the deadline, anyone may settle with the oracle's 4:00 PM price. The deadline length is not yet set. **Verdict on "no frozen money": Holds with a caveat.** The hold design itself is supported by reasoning and the plan's earlier research rather than by a finding in this run.

**1. The terms.**
- **Pause:** an admin switch that stops some functions for a while. **Unpause** turns them back on.
- **Freeze versus pause** in Aave, a lending protocol: "freeze" blocks new deposits and loans but still lets people withdraw, while "pause" blocks everything, withdrawals included (F5.58).
- **Pause guardian:** a separate key that can pause quickly while slower governance holds the other powers, as in Compound III (F5.35).
- **Close pair:** turn one Yes plus one No back into $1.00 (D27).
- **Settlement hold:** the plan's proposed delay on one market's settlement, with a deadline.

**2. Worked example.** At 1:00 PM the admin pauses META $680 because of a suspected bug. Alice holds 10 Yes (paid $6.50). Bob holds 10 No (net $3.50). Carol has a resting offer to buy 10 Yes at $0.60, which locks $6.00 of her USDC. Dave, a market maker, holds 5 Yes and 5 No.
- **Under the plan:** Bob can't mint, and nobody can place an order, so nothing trades, because a trade needs a new order to arrive. Carol can cancel and get her $6.00 back. Dave can close his 5 pairs for $5.00. The pause lasts past 4:00, but at 4:05 the bot settles anyway, since pause doesn't stop settlement. META closed at $684.00, Yes wins, and Alice redeems $10.00.
- **If the problem is the oracle instead:** suppose Pyth's 4:00 price reads $664.00 while the official close was $684.00. The admin, or the bot's pre-check, holds the market and proposes "Yes wins, official close $684.00" through the two-step override. If nobody acts before the 24-hour deadline, anyone may settle with Pyth's 4:00 price ($664.00, so No wins). The money is never stuck, but in that case the result would be wrong. The deadline guarantees the market ends, not that the answer is right.
- **Under an Aave-style full pause (option 3 below):** Alice couldn't redeem her $10.00 until someone unpauses. If the admin key were lost, it would be stuck forever, which breaks the spec's "redeemable indefinitely" (F5.61).

**3. The options and their evidence.**
- **(1) The spec read literally: stop minting and new orders, and leave every exit open.** This is the plan's choice. A paused Phoenix market (Phoenix is an on-chain order book on Solana) accepts "only reductions and withdrawals" (F5.36). Gnosis's conditional tokens, the design under Polymarket's tokens, have no pause check on merge or redeem (F5.39).
- **(2) Separate switches per function.** Drift has eight independent pause bits, including WithdrawPaused and FillPaused (F5.59). Compound III's guardian can pause five functions separately (F5.35). This would let you pause settlement for one market without pausing trading elsewhere, which is close to what the settlement hold does.
- **(3) Two tiers.** In Aave, "freeze" leaves exits open and "pause" blocks withdrawals too (F5.58). The second tier conflicts with the spec (F5.61).
- **(4) Nothing on the exit path can ever be paused.** This is Gnosis (F5.39), and it matches your direction.
- **(5) A permanent shutdown.** Maker's emergency shutdown was triggered by token holders, can't be undone, and is now deprecated (F5.60). It is too heavy for Meridian.
- **Questions the plan still leaves open:** whether a pause also cancels resting orders or just leaves them cancellable (the plan leaves them cancellable, like Phoenix, F5.36); whether unpausing should need a slower or different key than pausing (the guardian pattern, F5.35); and whether a pause can serve as the grace period after a devnet halt (F5.09, F5.62; see D19).

**4. What each answer commits you to.**
- **(1) with the hold (the plan):** tests that every exit still works while paused; the hold's deadline length; and a fallback price that is still available after a devnet halt (Conflict 5).
- **(2):** more switches to set, test and explain on screen.
- **(3) or (5):** a written departure from the spec's "redeemable indefinitely" and a much larger trust demand on the admin key.
- **The deadline length:** a short deadline (for example 2 hours) pays winners sooner but leaves less time to fix a wrong price. A long one (for example 24 hours, the plan's example) gives more time to catch errors but delays Alice's $10.00 on a held market.

**Recommendation (Claude's; yours to accept or reject).** Keep your direction and option (1). Before Architecture, set the deadline and decide who may place a hold (admin only, or the bot too). Give the public fallback a price source that survives a devnet halt. One reuse comes free: after a mid-day halt, a short pause lets market makers cancel stale orders before trading resumes.

### Q53. What should be logged on-chain, in the backend and in the browser?

You raised this on 2026-10-08. The three-layer answer is Claude's proposal. **Verdict: Your call.**

**1. The terms.**
- An **event** is a small record the program writes into a transaction's public log, such as "market settled: Yes". Anchor offers two kinds. `emit!` writes to the program's log and can be subscribed to live, but "data providers may truncate program logs", so events can be lost. `emit_cpi!` stores the event inside the transaction itself, costs a little more computation, is harder to lose, and can't be subscribed to directly: you fetch the transaction and decode it (F5.66, CONFIRMED).
- A **structured log** is a backend log line with named fields (job, market, attempt, result), so it can be searched.
- An **alert** is a message to a person (email, Slack, a webhook). A **dead man's switch** alerts when an expected check-in fails to arrive, which catches a job that never ran at all (F5.64).
- **Personal data** is information that can identify a person. Whether a wallet address counts is a legal question for group 7.
- **Slot lag:** how far a Solana server is behind the network. `getHealth` reports it (F5.14).

**2. Worked example.** At 4:05 the bot settles META $680.
- **On-chain event (public forever):** market META-680-2026-10-09, strike $680.00, Pyth price $684.00, confidence ±$0.05, publish time 16:00:00 ET, slot number, outcome Yes. Alice, a grader or anyone else can check it against Pyth.
- **Backend log line:** `job=settle market=META-680 attempt=1 price=684.00 conf=0.05 band=683.95..684.05 result=Yes tx=<signature> ms=1200`. It must never contain the Hermes key, for example inside a logged request header, or the bot's key.
- **Dead man's switch:** the job checks in to an outside watcher at 4:05:00 ("start") and 4:05:20 ("done"). If the bot had crashed at 3:50, no check-in arrives, and with a 5-minute grace period the admin is emailed at about 4:11 (illustrative numbers). The bot itself can't send that alert, because it is dead.
- **Browser:** Bob's wallet rejects a request. The site may report the error code, but under the plan it doesn't send Bob's wallet address to a third-party tracker without his consent.

**3. The options and their evidence.**
- **On-chain.** Events for create, add strike, mint, close pair, every fill, settle (with price, confidence, publish time and slot), hold, override propose/finalize (manual price plus a reason), pause and unpause, and config or key changes. Everything on-chain is public, so nothing secret or private goes in. The choice is `emit!` (simple and live, but can be trimmed) or `emit_cpi!` (sturdier, more work to read) (F5.66). Because Q27 makes fill events the source of P&L and History, a lost fill means a wrong P&L.
- **Backend.** A structured log for each job run; alerts; the bot's SOL and USDC balance; oracle confidence and price age; slot lag, plus a check that new blocks keep arriving, since `getHealth`'s format is marked unstable and no official devnet status page exists (F5.13, F5.14); and settlement time against the 10-minute target. OWASP: never log keys, tokens, connection strings or sensitive personal data, and always log authorization failures, start and stop, and any use of emergency powers such as the admin override (F5.63). A dead man's switch for "the job never ran" (F5.64).
- **Third-party notifications (optional).** Helius webhooks report transactions on watched addresses, but they promise no guaranteed delivery, may send duplicates, and switch themselves off at a 95% failure rate (F5.65). They can supplement your own watching but not replace it.
- **Browser.** Minimal error reporting with wallet-related data scrubbed and no secrets in the bundle. The plan adds no third-party tracking without consent. The default scrubbing of tools such as Sentry was not checked.
- **Scope.** The spec's monitoring line belongs to the mainnet bonus, but its automation section already asks the jobs to log results and alert on failure (F5.16 for settlement; the plan's §13 notes for the morning job). So job logs and alerts are core work, and fuller monitoring is bonus work.

**4. What each answer commits you to.**
- **`emit!` for fills:** a provider that doesn't trim logs, plus a way to rebuild P&L from the chain when an event is lost.
- **`emit_cpi!` for fills:** slightly more computation per fill, and the backend fetches and decodes each transaction instead of subscribing.
- **An outside watcher:** one more service to sign up for. Free-tier limits were not found.
- **Minimal browser logging:** less insight into user-side errors, in exchange for privacy, and a privacy notice for anything kept.

**Recommendation (Claude's; yours to accept or reject).** Keep the plan's three layers and add two things. Use `emit_cpi!`, or a reconciliation from account data, for the events that feed money figures (fills, settle, redeem). Add an outside dead man's switch for the 8:00, 8:30 and 4:05 jobs. Treat the rest of the monitoring as bonus scope.
