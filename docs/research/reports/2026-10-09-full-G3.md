### D6 (decision)

Leading answer. Compute strikes in integer fixed point, never floats. The oracle price is an integer mantissa with exponent -5, so apply the offsets as basis points (300, 600, 900, each up and down). Round half-up to the $10 grid, put the results in a set to deduplicate, and sort. Create one market per surviving strike. Keep the offsets and the $10 step in config, not hard-coded.

Market count at current MAG7 prices. These are my arithmetic from Pyth Terminal prices seen on 2026-10-09 around 15:47 ET. They are intraday, not closes, so treat them as indicative (F3.35). Prices: AAPL 337.09, MSFT 535.59, GOOGL 351.26, AMZN 261.92, NVDA 229.83, META 719.56, TSLA 384.11.
- Six offsets only: AAPL 6, MSFT 6, GOOGL 6, AMZN 5 (-3% and -6% both round to 250), NVDA 4 (-3% and -6% both give 220, and +3% and +6% both give 240), META 6, TSLA 6. Total 39 markets a day.
- With the rounded previous close as a 7th strike: 7, 7, 7, 6, 5, 7, 7. Total 46 a day.

What the admin may change. Add strikes before or during the day, and change offsets or step for future days through config. Do not edit or delete a market that has any minted supply, because that would change the contract under holders. The spec's own invariants (tokens only created by mint pair and only destroyed by redeem, outcome immutable) point the same way. Q33 covers the detail.

The $10 grid is coarse. For NVDA at about $230 one grid step is about 4.3%, so the ±3% and ±6% rungs collapse into the same strikes. Dedup is needed, not optional (F3.35, F3.24).

**Against:** Strongest case against. Fixed percentages on a fixed $10 grid give uneven ladders: 4 to 6 strikes per stock, and for any stock under roughly $330 the rungs collide. The ±9% tails are far from price and likely trade near $0 or $1, so they cost rent and add UI clutter for little trading. A volatility-scaled or per-ticker ladder, or a fixed number of strikes centred on the nearest grid point, would give every stock the same depth. Letting the admin freely 'adjust' strikes is also a trust risk for a product whose selling point is a fixed, auditable payoff. I found no venue data on trading volume by strike distance to settle this (gap). Kalshi lets traders request extra strikes that staff review (F3.25), which suggests that at least one venue adds strikes on demand instead of fixing the ladder in advance.

Findings: F3.08, F3.24, F3.25, F3.35, F3.26

### D7 (decision)

Leading answer. Keep the spec's timeline but make every time derive from that day's calendar entry (open and close), not from fixed clock times. All times are ET.
- Read the previous close: use the oracle value as of the prior session's close, a timestamped historical read, not 'latest' (F3.06, F3.07).
- Compute strikes and create markets: 8:00 to 8:30.
- Markets visible and minting enabled: 9:00.
- Live trading: 9:30.
- Trading stops at the session close (4:00 PM, or 1:00 PM on early-close days).
- Settlement: close + about 5 minutes.
- Redemption: opens when settled, then stays open indefinitely.

What can slip without harm:
- The previous-close read and the strike calculation can slip freely until market creation starts, because yesterday's close cannot change. They could even run the prior evening.
- Market creation can slip from 8:30 up to 9:00, when markets become visible.
- The 9:00 minting switch can slip up to 9:30.
- Redemption timing has no deadline.
- Settlement can slip a little. The spec's own bar is within 10 minutes of the close (about 4:10), the retry window is 15 minutes, and the admin override is available after 1 hour.

What cannot slip: the trading cutoff at the close, because trading after the outcome is known is harmful. Also note that the Core oracle feed for equities has a regular session of 09:30 to 16:00 only (F3.02, F3.06). An 8:00 AM 'latest' read will therefore not be the close unless the call is by timestamp. A 4:05 read of a feed whose session ended at 4:00 may breach a 5-minute staleness rule (F3.32, my inference). Whatever staleness rule is chosen has to be measured against the close time, not against 'now'.

**Against:** Strongest case against. The 8:00 read and 8:30 create steps are an arbitrary 30-minute gap that exists only because the spec says so. Computing strikes right after the prior day's settlement, or at about 8:00 in one job, removes a dependency on the morning oracle read. It also makes a morning oracle outage visible hours earlier. Against 'settle at close + 5 minutes': a fixed 4:05 may be wrong if the official closing auction print differs from the oracle's last aggregate. Exact oracle-versus-official close behaviour is unverified, and the settlement-read problem belongs to another group's oracle items. Polymarket's daily stock markets open at 8:00 AM ET on the prior day (F3.27), so they take a different approach, but that page contradicts itself and the finding is unconfirmed.

Findings: F3.01, F3.02, F3.03, F3.06, F3.07, F3.27, F3.32

### D12 (decision)

Leading answer. Use a version-controlled static calendar file as the source of truth. It holds the NYSE-published holidays and early closes (1:00 PM ET), with a test that fails when the next 12 months are missing. Add two runtime guards:
- Cross-check against the oracle's own feed schedule and live is_open / next_close fields (F3.02).
- Alert, and skip the day, on any disagreement.

What each source gives for 2026 and 2027:
- NYSE hours-and-calendars page: lists 2026, 2027 and 2028. Closed: 2026 Jan 1, Jan 19, Feb 16, Apr 3, May 25, Jun 19, Jul 3, Sep 7, Nov 26, Dec 25; 2027 Jan 1, Jan 18, Feb 15, Mar 26, May 31, Jun 18, Jul 5, Sep 6, Nov 25, Dec 24. Early closes: 2026-11-27, 2026-12-24, 2027-11-26. Dec 31, 2027 stays open (New Year's 2028 not observed). Primary, but there is no machine-readable feed (F3.01).
- Pyth Hermes feed metadata: a 'schedule' string with holiday and early-close overrides. It matches NYSE exactly for every date it lists, but it lists only up to 07-05 (2027). It has no Sep 6 2027, Nov 25 or 26 2027, or Dec 24 2027 yet. It is a rolling window, so it is not enough alone for 2027 (F3.02, F3.33).
- exchange_calendars (Python): community-maintained. I could not confirm its 2026/2027 contents (F3.04).
- Broker calendar APIs (Alpaca and similar): not verified. The docs URLs I tried returned 404 (gap).

Unscheduled closures (national days of mourning, disasters) are not in any pre-published calendar. Only a live signal catches them (F3.05).

**Against:** Strongest case against. A hard-coded file must be refreshed by hand every year, and a missed refresh would silently break market creation. A maintained library or API refreshes itself. Using the oracle's schedule as the calendar would keep creation and settlement aligned with exactly what the oracle will serve. That matters most on early-close days (F3.02). Its weakness is the rolling window, so it needs an authoritative backstop, and the Hermes price endpoints now need a paid or trial key (F3.03). The NYSE page is also not a stable API, and I could not open the Nasdaq page (timed out) or the broker calendar docs to compare. Unscheduled-closure notice periods are unverified (F3.05).

Findings: F3.01, F3.02, F3.03, F3.04, F3.05, F3.33

### D29 (decision)

Leading answer. Beyond the morning and settlement jobs, the product needs the following. The first four are scheduled jobs and the rest are always-on.
1. A calendar sync/validation job (daily, before the morning job) that confirms today is a session day and its close time (D12).
2. An open-minting step at 9:00, unless minting is gated by an on-chain start time. Prefer the on-chain gate so no job can be missed.
3. A trading-cutoff step at the close, only if the order book cannot enforce a cutoff itself (Q14).
4. A settlement reconciler. After the 15-minute retry window, any market not yet settled raises an alert and queues admin override. The spec says 'alert admin' but defines no catch-up job.
5. An always-on health monitor: automation wallet SOL balance (rent is consumed daily, Q10), RPC and oracle reachability, and alert delivery.
6. An always-on event indexer for the History and Portfolio pages (the spec's frontend needs a trade log).
7. An order-book crank, but only if the chosen CLOB needs one. Phoenix and Manifest are crankless and OpenBook is not (F3.31).

Time zones and DST. Define every schedule in the IANA zone America/New_York and store and compare instants in UTC. Do not use fixed-UTC cron: 8:00 ET is 12:00 UTC in summer and 13:00 UTC in winter. DST changes at 2:00 AM on a Sunday (2026: Mar 8 and Nov 1; 2027: Mar 14 and Nov 7), when markets are closed. The 8:00 and 16:05 jobs never fall in the gap, and croner and node-cron skip gap times and run overlaps once (F3.21, F3.23). Early-close days move the settlement time to about 13:05, so compute run times from the calendar each day instead of a static cron string. Do not use GitHub Actions cron for the settlement job. It supports a timezone but can be delayed under load and has a 5-minute minimum interval (F3.22).

**Against:** Strongest case against. The spec says two jobs. Extra scheduled jobs add failure modes. A single idempotent reconciler that runs every minute and compares desired state (from the calendar) with on-chain state would remove missed-run problems after a restart. It would also need no cron strings or DST handling, and would replace items 1 to 4 entirely. Counter to adding an indexer: History could be read straight from chain history and the order book instead of keeping a database, which keeps the app simpler. Which jobs are really needed depends on the CLOB and oracle choices, which this review keeps blind, so item 7 is conditional.

Findings: F3.02, F3.21, F3.22, F3.23, F3.31, F3.15, F3.16

### Q8 (open question)

Options.
(A) Offer the rounded previous close as a 7th strike. Evidence:
- It is the 'will it close above yesterday's close' contract, the point where the Yes price is nearest 50 cents and a binary is most uncertain. The spec says the most active trading is near the current price.
- Cboe's own 0DTE page says near-the-money options are 'extremely sensitive' to moves into expiry (F3.29), which is qualitative support that attention concentrates at the money.
- It adds 7 markets a day at current prices (39 to 46, about +18%) and the rent for each (F3.35, F3.36).
- Today it never collides with a ±3% strike for any of the seven stocks, but it would for stocks below about $330 where the grid is coarse.
(B) Offer only the six percentage strikes. Evidence: the nearest strike to yesterday's close is within about $5 of it anyway (AAPL 340, NVDA 230, META 720). The ±3% rung already covers the area for high-priced stocks such as META and MSFT. It saves 7 markets and rent.
(C) Add the near-the-money strike on demand, using the admin Add Strike function.

I could not find any published volume-by-strike-distance data for binary or 0DTE markets (gap). Polymarket runs daily stock 'Up or Down' markets, which are close-versus-reference binaries. Its pages contradict themselves on the reference price and source, so that comparison is unconfirmed (F3.27).

**Against:** Evidence that would change the picture: volume or open-interest by strike distance from a comparable venue (Kalshi daily ladders, Cboe 0DTE strike-level data), or devnet testing that shows whether liquidity actually clusters at the previous-close strike. If the stock gaps at the open (Q34), the previous-close strike stops being the nearest-to-price strike, and that weakens the argument for (A). If the project cares about rent and market count, (B) or (C) wins. If the product wants a simple headline contract, (A) wins.

Findings: F3.08, F3.29, F3.35, F3.36, F3.27

### Q9 (open question)

The spec says strikes sit at 'user-determined percentage intervals', which could mean one of five things.
(1) Fixed constants in code (3, 6, 9). This is what the spec algorithm shows.
(2) Operator-configurable offsets stored in on-chain config or env, applied the same to all tickers.
(3) Per-ticker offsets that reflect different volatility.
(4) Trader-requested strikes reviewed by staff. Kalshi runs exactly this: the market page has a 'Request Additional Strikes' tab and its markets team reviews each request (F3.25). That shows one venue treating the traders as the 'users' who help set strikes.
(5) Fixed spacing set per instrument with the ladder re-centred. Nadex used this: one at-the-money strike plus a fixed number of strikes above and below at a set interval per instrument, narrower at some times of day (F3.30, SECONDARY).

Evidence on size: Pyth Terminal showed 24-hour moves of -0.97%, +2.47%, +3.11%, +2.44%, -0.30%, +0.86% and -0.16% for the seven stocks on 2026-10-09. So ±3% sits inside ordinary one-day moves for several of these names and ±9% sits far out (F3.08). I did not find a source for the daily volatility statistics of each stock, which would size the ladder properly (gap).

**Against:** Evidence that would change the picture: whoever wrote the spec saying what 'user' meant (the operator, or the person trading); realised daily-return distributions for each MAG7 stock showing whether ±9% is ever reached; and any hint that frontend users would use a request feature. If 'user-determined' means 'the project owner sets them once', option (1) with config is enough and the rest is extra scope.

Findings: F3.25, F3.30, F3.08

### Q10 (open question)

Who pays. On Solana the payer signs and funds rent; the payer of an associated token account can differ from the owner and from the fee payer (F3.12). The options are:
(1) The automation wallet pays all market-creation rent and fees. This is the default, since the automation service creates the markets.
(2) Users pay their own token accounts and fees.
(3) Someone sponsors user accounts and fees through a separate payer (possible, but extra infrastructure).

Amounts. Rent is (data bytes + 128) x 6,960 lamports (F3.11) and the base fee is 5,000 lamports per signature (F3.14). My arithmetic (F3.36):
- Token account (165 bytes): 0.00203928 SOL (F3.12).
- Mint (82 bytes, a standard size I did not re-open): 0.0014616 SOL each. Two mints (Yes and No) are about 0.0029 SOL per market.
- Vault token account: 0.00204 SOL.
- Program market-state account: say 300 bytes, about 0.003 SOL. The size is unknown.
- Total before the order book: roughly 0.008 SOL per market, or about 0.3 to 0.37 SOL a day for 39 to 46 markets.
- Transaction fees: trivial, at most a few thousandths of a SOL a day.

The order book dominates, and it depends on which CLOB. Manifest's README lists creation rent as 0.007 SOL, OpenBook 2 SOL, and Phoenix 3+ SOL (F3.15; this is Manifest's own comparison and I could not verify it independently). At those rates:
- Manifest class: about 0.27 to 0.32 SOL a day more.
- OpenBook: about 78 to 92 SOL a day.
- Phoenix: 117 SOL or more a day.

The faucet gives 'maximum of 2 requests every 8 hours' (F3.16), so the larger figures are not fundable on devnet.

Rent recovery. The classic Token program cannot close mint accounts, so each day's 2 mints strand about 0.003 SOL permanently. Token-2022 with MintCloseAuthority can close mints (F3.13). Token accounts (vaults, users' accounts) can be closed to recover rent.

Users per strike traded: about 0.004 SOL for Yes and No token accounts, plus a one-off USDC account.

**Against:** Evidence that would change the picture: the actual account sizes and rent for the chosen CLOB and program, which can be read by calling getMinimumBalanceForRentExemption, and whether the program can close accounts and return rent. Also whether the project uses Token-2022 instead of the classic Token program. The Manifest comparison is partisan, so Phoenix and OpenBook costs should be checked in their own docs (not found). Public devnet faucet limits can also change.

Findings: F3.11, F3.12, F3.13, F3.14, F3.15, F3.16, F3.36

### Q14 (open question)

Early-close dates (1:00 PM ET): 2026-11-27, 2026-12-24 and 2027-11-26. NYSE also lists 1:15 PM for eligible options, which does not affect a stock-close contract (F3.01). The oracle's own schedule uses 09:30 to 13:00 on 1127 and 1224 (F3.02).

Options:
(A) Derive everything from the calendar. Creation (8:00 to 8:30), visibility (9:00) and open (9:30) are unchanged. The trading cutoff becomes 1:00 PM, and settlement about 1:05 PM. This matches the spec's 'settle within 10 minutes of close' wording.
(B) Skip early-close days (no markets). This is simple but loses 3 trading days a year.
(C) Trade until 4:00 PM and settle at 4:05 as usual. This leaves a 3-hour window where the outcome is known and the oracle feed is closed. Trading after the outcome is known is harmful, and a settlement read would be about 3 hours stale (F3.32, my inference).
(D) Treat it like a normal day. Polymarket's rules reportedly treat early closes as normal sessions with the official shortened close (F3.28, SECONDARY, from a search snippet that I did not open).

**Against:** Evidence that would change the picture: whether the program can enforce a trading cutoff on-chain at all (if the CLOB cannot, (A) needs a cutoff job). Whether the oracle publishes a clean close print on the shortened session. The user's preference for coverage versus simplicity. The 'official close' on shortened days comes from the exchange closing auction, which may differ slightly from the oracle's last aggregate (not verified).

### Q32 (open question)

Halfway cases are real: a $500 previous close gives -3% = $485.00, -9% = $455, +3% = $515 and +9% = $545, which are all exactly halfway (F3.37). The rule matters because the at-or-above settlement rule makes 'which neighbour' change the market.

Rounding rules:
- Half-up (for positive prices the same as 'half away from zero'): 485 to 490, 455 to 460, 515 to 520, 545 to 550. Rust f64::round does this; JavaScript Math.round does too for positive values, since it rounds halves toward +infinity (F3.24).
- Half-even ('banker's rounding'): 485 to 480, 455 to 460, 515 to 520, 545 to 540. Python's round() and Rust f64::round_ties_even do this (F3.24).
- Round half down, or floor/ceiling: not standard and would need justification.

Number format. Floating-point is the risk: a product like 0.97 x 500 is not exactly representable, so a true halfway case may land on either side of .5. The oracle already returns an integer mantissa with exponent -5 (F3.08), so integer or decimal arithmetic can be exact: convert to integer cents, apply basis points, round with an explicit rule. The spec's AAPL example (230 gives 210, 220, 220, 230, 240, 240, 250) does not contain a halfway case, so it does not settle this.

**Against:** Evidence that would change the picture: how often exact halves occur in practice (needs a price distribution; rare but deterministic, so a test fixture should include $500). Whether the on-chain program or only the off-chain service computes strikes. If only off-chain, the chain never sees the rule, but any independent checker will need the same rule documented. Both common rules are defensible, so the choice is a convention more than a fact.

Findings: F3.24, F3.08, F3.37

### Q33 (open question)

The spec function list has Add Strike only, but the prose says 'add or adjust strikes before or during the trading day'. What adjusting could mean for a market people already hold:
(1) Adding only. No existing market is touched. Matches the function list. Kalshi works this way: traders request extra strikes and staff add them (F3.25). I found no source showing it editing a listed strike.
(2) Replace or remove a strike before any pair is minted (zero supply). The accounts can be closed and rent reclaimed except classic mints (F3.13). Safe, since nobody holds anything.
(3) After minting: treat the market as fixed. Options are to pause it, let it run to settlement, or void it with a refund path. A refund path does not exist in the spec: tokens are 'only destroyed via the redeem function' and the outcome is immutable once written.
(4) Change the strike under holders. This changes the contract after people bought it. Polymarket's docs say clarifications 'cannot change the fundamental intent of the question' (F3.26), which is the venue norm against this.

The options-exchange convention (strikes only change for corporate actions, via OCC adjustment) is not verified; I could not open the OCC or Cboe rule text (gap).

**Against:** Evidence that would change the picture: venue or exchange rules that allow delisting or editing a series with open interest, which I could not find. Also whether the program has any void-and-refund path and whether the spec's invariants permit adding one. If the project wants a 'cancel market' emergency tool, it needs a refund design reviewed against the vault invariant (vault balance equals $1.00 x pairs minted).

Findings: F3.25, F3.26, F3.13

### Q34 (open question)

If a stock gaps (after earnings), most or all fixed ladders end up on one side. Worked example (F3.37): NVDA previous close $229.83, a 10% gap down to about $207. The strikes (210, 220, 230, 240, 250) are all above the new price. Only 210 is close, and there is no strike below $207 at all, so nobody can trade 'will it close above $200'.

Options:
(a) Keep the previous-close ladder and let the admin add strikes after the gap shows (the spec's Add Strike).
(b) Delay or re-centre creation using a pre-market price shortly before 9:00.
(c) Add rungs automatically whenever the live price leaves the ladder.
(d) Create a wider ladder on known earnings days.

Does the oracle give a pre-market price? Pyth sources disagree on this, so I leave it open:
- The Hermes feed metadata for the Core Equity.US feeds lists a regular session only: weekdays 09:30 to 16:00 (F3.02). Core docs say equity feeds 'follow traditional market hours' and outside those hours 'it's not clear what an equity's price is' (F3.06).
- Pyth Terminal feed pages (the numbered feeds) list 24/5 coverage including pre-market 04:00 to 09:30, post-market and overnight (F3.08). Pyth Pro docs expose a marketSession field (regular, preMarket, postMarket, overNight, closed). Pro requires a subscription (F3.07).
- So a pre-market price exists in Pyth Pro, and probably not in Core. Whether an on-chain Core read can return it is unconfirmed.

**Against:** Evidence that would change the picture: confirmation that Core publishes pre-market prices on Solana (would make (b) cheap), or that Pro's subscription fits the project's budget. A non-Pyth pre-market source (a broker or data API) would also change it, and I did not verify one. Pre-market prices are thin and can mislead. Also whether trading starts at 9:30 anyway: a gap is partly visible by 9:00 from news, so (a) may be enough if the admin is watching.

Findings: F3.02, F3.06, F3.07, F3.08, F3.37

### Q35 (open question)

Share classes. Pyth lists GOOG (Class C) and GOOGL (Class A) as separate feeds with different feed IDs: GOOGL 5a48c03e...5aa6, GOOG e65ff435...f3f2 (F3.09). The spec names GOOGL, so a strike ladder for GOOGL has to read the GOOGL feed. The feed ID should be pinned in config and checked against its description ('ALPHABET INC CLASS A') on every start, so that nobody swaps in GOOG by mistake.

Stock splits. A split changes the share price by a fixed ratio at the ex-date open while the oracle's previous close can still be the old price. Worked example (F3.37): a hypothetical 10-for-1 split on a $1,200 stock would trade near $120, but strikes from the unadjusted close would be $1,092 to $1,308, all far above the price, so every market would be a near-certain No.

Options:
(a) Watch a corporate-actions calendar and skip that ticker for the day (the admin decides).
(b) Sanity-check the previous close against a second price (a pre-market read or a second source) and skip on a large mismatch.
(c) Rely on split-adjusted history from the oracle. Pyth's core docs do not say how splits are treated (F3.10), but a Polymarket rule reportedly says split-adjusted prices as displayed on Pyth are used if a split happens (SECONDARY snippet).

No announced split was found for any of the seven names as of today. In early 2026, articles speculated about Microsoft and Meta, and noted the others have already split (F3.10, SECONDARY).

**Against:** Evidence that would change the picture: Pyth documentation or feed behaviour on a past split (for example how the feed handled earlier MAG7 splits), which I could not find. Also whether a split's ex-date normally falls after announcement with enough notice for (a) to be reliable. If the likelihood of a split is low, the cheapest option is a sanity check plus a manual skip. The feed metadata alone does not show how adjustment works.

Findings: F3.09, F3.10, F3.37

### Q42 (open question)

Retry design options and evidence.
- Make every create call idempotent. If the market address is derived from ticker, date and strike, a retry that finds the account already exists can count as success. This is design advice from me, not from a source.
- Back off exponentially with jitter. AWS calls jittered backoff 'a standard approach for remote clients' and says Full Jitter does less work while Decorrelated Jitter is slightly faster (F3.19).
- Before re-signing, check that the old transaction's blockhash has expired. The Solana guide puts expiry at 150 blocks, about 79 seconds, and says it is 'very important' to confirm expiry first, because otherwise both transactions could land (F3.18).
- Bound retries by a deadline (9:00 when markets become visible) rather than a count.

Limits on public devnet, per IP (F3.17): 100 requests per 10 seconds in total, 40 per 10 seconds for any single method, 40 concurrent connections, 40 new connections per 10 seconds, 100 MB per 30 seconds; 'subject to change'. A burst of 39 to 46 creates can stay under 40 per 10 seconds if sends are paced at about 3 to 4 a second, but confirmation polling, blockhash and account reads share the 100-per-10-second total. Settlement is a similar burst of 39 to 46 settle calls, and shared IPs (a developer, the frontend, the service) share the same budget.

Transaction limits (F3.14, F3.20): 1,232 bytes, 64 accounts, 200,000 compute units by default, 1.4 million at most. Creating mints, vault, state and the order book in a single transaction may not fit, so a market may take several steps, which multiplies the request count.

Oracle side: Hermes accepts multiple feed IDs in one request (F3.34), so settlement needs 7 price fetches, not 46. The Hermes API needs a key since 2026-08-26 (F3.03). I did not find its current rate limit; an older doc said 30 requests per 10 seconds per IP with a 60-second block (SECONDARY).

**Against:** Evidence that would change the picture: measured burst behaviour on devnet at 8:00 AM and 4:05 PM ET. The public endpoint is shared and 'subject to change', and a dedicated provider endpoint would remove the limits (not verified). Also the actual number of transactions each market needs, which depends on the CLOB. If the whole market is created in one transaction, the burst is 39 to 46 sends and the limits are not a problem.

Findings: F3.03, F3.14, F3.17, F3.18, F3.19, F3.20, F3.34

### F3.24 [Q32] [D6] [S12] CONFIRMED

- Lens: technical
- Source: [Math.round (MDN)](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Global_Objects/Math/round): "If the fractional portion is exactly 0.5, the argument is rounded to the next integer in the direction of +∞."
- Source: [f64 (Rust std)](https://doc.rust-lang.org/std/primitive.f64.html#method.round): "If a value is half-way between two integers, round away from 0.0."
- Verification (2026-10-09): held: Opened MDN Math.round: exactly 0.5 rounds toward +infinity, and Math.round(-5.5) gives -5. Opened Rust f64 docs: round sends halves away from 0.0 (3.5 gives 4.0, 4.5 gives 5.0). round_ties_even gives 4.0 for both 3.5 and 4.5.
